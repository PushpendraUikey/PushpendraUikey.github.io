For most of 2026, the AI story only moved in one direction. More chips, more data centers, higher valuations, and a constant drumbeat that traditional software and IT services were living on borrowed time.

The last two weeks broke that script. Nvidia had its worst stretch in years and lost its CEO. Indian IT stocks, written off for most of the year, suddenly became the market's favourite hiding place. Frontier labs started selling the kind of security work that services firms bill by the hour. And back home, Paytm got a second shot at the wallet business on terms that say a lot about where Indian fintech is headed.

I think these are all the same story: the market is figuring out who actually captures the value from AI, and who just pays for it.

## Nvidia crashed, Indian IT rallied. That isn't a comeback.

In early October, Nvidia was worth roughly $5.8 trillion and looked untouchable. Then the cracks came in layers. First, doubts about whether the world's AI spending would ever pay back. Then the US midterms, which raised fears that new Democratic majorities would make data centers harder to build. Then a board investigation into Jensen Huang, ending in his resignation on November 12. By the end of that week, Nvidia was down around the $4 trillion mark and no longer the world's most valuable company.

The interesting part is where the money went. Nifty IT, the worst sector on Dalal Street for most of 2026, rose about 18% in October and another ~13.5% through November 12. TCS, Infosys and Mphasis were suddenly being bought as the "anti-AI trade".

I find that ironic. Earlier this year, these same stocks were sold off *because of* AI, every time a new tool promised to automate legacy modernisation or routine coding work. Now they're bought as a hedge against AI. Both can't be the long-term story.

My read is that this is a flows story, not a fundamentals story. Indian IT is being bought because it isn't Nvidia. I haven't seen anything new about deal pipelines, pricing power or the headcount math of AI-assisted delivery. Only the relative valuation changed.

Then Nvidia reported on November 17, its first quarter under interim CEO Colette Kress. Revenue came in at $115.8 billion, up 103% year on year and ahead of estimates, with guidance of about $130 billion for the next quarter. The business behind the AI build-out is clearly still growing fast; what broke was confidence, not demand.

That matters for the IT rotation. If the AI trade recovers, some of the money that sheltered in Indian IT may simply leave again. Whether the rally sticks depends on whether these companies can show AI turning into revenue for them, not just away from someone else.

## AI agents are becoming the security team

On November 12, two announcements landed on the same day. At its "Cyber at the Frontier" event, OpenAI showed fleets of AI agents that pen-test web applications using their source code. Anthropic put a self-hosted edition of Claude Managed Agents into private beta, so enterprises can run agents inside their own cloud or data centre.

Taken together, the frontier labs are walking straight into work that cybersecurity firms, and the cyber practices of IT majors, have sold by the hour for years: finding vulnerabilities, validating exploits, writing up reports.

The "self-hosted" part is what makes this real for banks, governments and anyone in a regulated industry. The barrier was never the capability; it was the idea of sending sensitive code and credentials to someone else's servers.

OpenAI's approach is also telling. Its most capable cyber model sits behind a gated Daybreak Red tier for vetted defenders, and approved service providers can embed its models into their own managed services. The labs are not trying to replace security firms outright. They want those firms as distribution.

The squeeze falls on manual pen-testing shops, entry-level SOC analyst roles, and any IT services cyber practice whose margin comes from headcount. My prediction: security services move from "we find your bugs" to "we run, supervise and are accountable for the agents that find your bugs". The firms that become trusted operators of these gated models will win, not the ones with the most analysts.

## Paytm's second act, and the RBI's new rule: prove you're boring first

On November 13, Paytm's payments subsidiary received in-principle approval from the RBI to run a wallet business again. The catch: final approval depends on a system and cybersecurity audit, and the report has to reach the RBI within six months or the approval lapses.

On its own, that's a redemption headline. Next to the rest of the RBI's week, it reads more like a statement of intent. The central bank proposed limits on private equity firms controlling multiple NBFCs. It consolidated its rules on domestic money transfers. And NPCI launched UPI Vyapaar Saksham to push digital payments among small merchants. Tighter governance on one side, wider access on the other.

The macro backdrop doesn't make life easier. October retail inflation came in at 6.09%, above the top of the RBI's tolerance band for the first time in two years, and a December rate hike is now a live possibility. For fintech lenders, that means a higher cost of funds just as the regulator is asking harder questions.

My thesis: Indian fintech has finished moving from "move fast" to "earn permission". Paytm's second chance is the clearest sign yet that audits, governance and boring compliance are now the moat, not user growth. The fintechs that thrive from here will look less like startups and more like well-run banks with better apps.

## The common thread

Three very different headlines, one shift. The easy narratives of 2026 (AI wins everything, services lose everything, fintech grows at any cost) are giving way to a harder question: who has the trust, the distribution and the accountability to turn new technology into durable revenue? Nvidia still has the demand. Indian IT still has to prove its AI story. Security firms have to become operators. And fintechs have to become boring. I'll be watching all four.
